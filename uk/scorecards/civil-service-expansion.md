## C. Counterfactual

**Method used:** Direct accounting with scenario analysis

**Baseline:** March 2019 (pre-COVID, after initial post-austerity recovery). FTE: 370,900.

**Key assumptions:**
- COVID-specific functions (Test & Trace, furlough administration) were temporary. The Office for National Statistics estimated approximately 20,000–25,000 roles created for pandemic response were intended to be wound down.
- Brexit created genuine permanent demand for border, customs, and regulatory roles (widely estimated at 15,000–20,000 additional FTE).
- Efficiency gains from technology should, over time, offset headcount demand growth.
- Salary inflation tracks CPIH over this period (CPIH rose ~23% 2019–2024).

**Central estimate:** A civil service maintained at 2019 levels in FTE terms, adjusted for Brexit-specific demand (+17,500 FTE), would have employed approximately 388,400 FTE in 2024 vs the actual 506,100 — a gap of approximately **117,700 FTE** (23% above counterfactual).

At the average 2024 staff cost of £37,800/FTE, this represents approximately **£4.4 billion per year in excess staffing cost**, relative to maintaining a Brexit-adjusted 2019 baseline.

**Sensitivity range:**
- Low (Brexit + Covid permanent roles justified): counterfactual FTE 420,000, excess cost ~£3.3bn/year
- Central: excess cost ~£4.4bn/year
- High (2017 trough trajectory maintained): excess cost ~£5.6bn/year

**Confidence in counterfactual:** MEDIUM  
*Causal attribution is partial — the expansion reflects multiple simultaneous policy choices across different departments, not a single controllable variable.*

## G. Verdict

**Fiscal score:** Negative  
**Economic score:** Negative  
**Delivery score:** Partially achieved  

**Bottom line:**

> The UK civil service grew by 41% between its 2017 trough and March 2024, ending the decade of stated austerity commitment with a headcount 10% above the 2010 peak. This expansion cost an estimated £4.4 billion more per year in staff costs than a Brexit-adjusted 2019 baseline. The expansion delivered identifiable outputs — principally the COVID response — but the post-pandemic wind-down did not occur. ONS measures of public sector productivity show output broadly flat against the rising input cost. No government has produced a public justification for a civil service of 542,000 — the size emerged from accumulated departmental decisions rather than deliberate design. Evidence quality is HIGH on headcount and MEDIUM on cost and productivity. The question of whether 542,000 civil servants deliver materially more than 390,000 did has not been answered by any government-commissioned evaluation.

## Editor's note

The civil service expansion is not a scandal in the conventional sense — the growth reflects real demand created by COVID and Brexit. The question nstate asks is not whether individual hiring decisions were wrong but whether the *cumulative outcome* — a civil service larger than at any point since the 1980s, with no explicit mandate, growing faster than public sector productivity — represents value for money. That question has not been asked in public, and the data suggests it should be.

The most important missing piece is a department-by-department breakdown of where the 158,000 additional FTE went. Cabinet Office publishes this by department but analysing which departments drove growth, and whether their functions justify it, would require 15 separate sub-scorecards. That analysis is a candidate for a contributor project.
